# 1. What Kinds of Thing Does a Statute Book Name?

The unit being counted is a **heading segment**, not necessarily a legal act. A section may combine several subjects with “--,” as in §10-1-403, “**Levy of telecommunications license tax -- Recovery from customers -- Enactment, repeal, or change in rate of tax -- Annexation**.” Consequently, the following percentages are **estimates from the 2,044-heading sample, calibrated by the full-population leading-word table**. Categories are assigned by the heading’s primary function and therefore conceal some overlap.

| Primary subject of heading | Estimated share | Evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Definitions, titles, scope, applicability, construction | **15%** | “Definitions” alone leads 2,022 full-population segments; “Title” 674; “Scope” 164; “Applicability” 156. Examples: §11-55-102, “**Definitions**”; §13-56-101, “**Title**”; §13-47-103, “**Scope of chapter**”; §10-1-103, “**Construction**.” |

**Estimated act-shaped share: approximately 35–45%.** Even that includes government-only acts, procedural steps, omissions, and enforcement acts—not merely voluntary transactions. Only roughly **15–20% of all headings** appear to name the kinds of affirmative private legal acts suggested by strings such as “amend-operating-agreement.” The statute book primarily names **norms, institutions, statuses, procedures, and consequences surrounding acts**, not acts alone.

# 3. Dimensions That Distinguish Legal Acts

## (a) Performer: accept, but use a role lattice

This is indispensable. Compare an act open to a private individual—§77-7-3, “**Temporary detention made by a private individual**”—with a licensed-role act, §58-17b-302, “**License required -- License classifications for pharmacy facilities**”; a fiduciary act, §75A-7-105, “**Assignment of security by a fiduciary**”; and a sovereign act, §10-5-112, “**Property tax levy set by ordinance**.”

The axis should distinguish **natural person, organization, officeholder, licensed professional, fiduciary, court, agency, and legislative body**, while permitting multiple authorized roles.

## (b) Changes a legal relation versus operates within one: accept and refine

Constitutive acts change status or relations: merger under §16-1a-702, “**Merger authorized**,” or severance under §57-1-5, “**Severance of joint tenancy**.” Performance acts operate inside an existing relation: delivery under §70A-2-307, “**Delivery in single lot or several lots**,” and payment under §11-1-104, “**Form, time, and place of payment**.”

Add a third value: **invokes institutional machinery**, such as filing a petition under §11-30-3 or requesting judicial review under §26B-9-230.

## (c) Hohfeldian effect: accept, but not as the only effect model

The corpus supports creation, transfer, extinction, and alteration of rights, powers, duties, liabilities, and immunities:

- right extinguished: §70A-1a-306, “**Waiver or renunciation of claim or right**”;
- power created: §16-16-822, “**Appointment and authority of officers**”;
- duty imposed: §23A-5-207, “**Exhibition of license … required**”;
- immunity created: §53-2d-802, “**Immunity**”;
- liability created: §57-8-58, “**Liability of declarant or management committee**.”

Refinement is necessary because legal acts also change **status, priority, jurisdiction, evidence, and public-record effect**, which are not comfortably reduced to one Hohfeldian incident.

## (d) Formality: accept as a bundle, not a scale

Writing, signature, acknowledgment, filing, recording, registration, publication, notice, and certification are independent predicates. Compare §47-1-309, “**Notice to be displayed -- Disclosure to be provided in writing**”; §77-2-2.2, “**Signing and filing of information**”; §75-13-206, “**Notarization and acknowledgment**”; and §38-13-201, “**Lien on aircraft -- Filing required -- Notice -- Release**.”

A single “high/low formality” score would lose which formality supplies validity, perfection, evidence, or notice.

## (e) Counterparty assent: accept, but distinguish assent from receipt and approval

Merger and contract formation generally involve assent, while recording a lien does not. Some acts require **approval rather than counterparty assent**, as in §17C-5-304, “**Consent of each taxing entity … required**.” Others require only delivery or notice, such as §20A-5-403.5, “**Ballot drop boxes -- Notice**.” Values should include unilateral, notice-dependent, acceptance-dependent, consent-dependent, approval-dependent, and adjudicated.

## (f) Revocability: accept as temporal doctrine

The source expressly distinguishes revocation and amendment: §75A-4-206, “**Donor’s power to revoke or amend**”; §31A-16-115, “**Revocation, suspension, or nonrenewal of insurer’s license**.” The axis must include **freely revocable, revocable on conditions, irrevocable, voidable, appealable, and final**. “Final permit decision … Appeal” in §40-14-203 demonstrates that finality and reviewability are separate.

## (g) Third-party effect: accept

Perfection and recording make acts effective against outsiders: §70A-9a-302, “**Law governing perfection and priority of agricultural liens**.” By contrast, §15-3-2, “**Contracts not discharged by merger in obligor and obligee**,” concerns relations principally among identified parties. Values should include inter partes, successor-binding, creditor-binding, public/erga omnes, and evidentiary only.

## Additional axes revealed by the corpus

1. **Institutional channel:** private instrument, registry filing, agency application, legislative enactment, or judicial order.  
2. **Legal stage:** initiation, notice, investigation, decision, review, enforcement, termination—illustrated by §13-14-304, “**Hearing regarding termination**,” and §40-14-203, “**Final permit decision … Appeal**.”  
3. **Validity consequence:** void, voidable, ineffective against third parties, sanctionable, or merely defective. §78B-5-812 names a settlement “**When voidable**”; §70A-9a-704 names a security interest “**unperfected**.”  
4. **Temporal effect:** immediate, effective on filing, effective on notice, prospective, retroactive, or effective at death. Compare §48-2e-206, “**Effective time and date**,” §19-6-402.5, “**Retroactive effect**,” and §75-6-413, “**Effect … at transferor’s death**.”  
5. **Object type:** person, office, status, claim, instrument, property, organization, territory, public rule, or adjudication.  
6. **Modality:** permitted, mandatory, prohibited, excused, immune, or sanctioned—not merely act identity.

# 5. Telos Versus Effect

**Position: purpose and legal effect must be orthogonal facets. A purpose-rooted hierarchy cannot serve as the normative identity system for legal acts.**

The same institutional form crosses purposes. “Registration” covers doing business (§16-1a-503, “**Registration to do business in this state**”), voting (§20A-2-101, “**Eligibility for registration**”), motor vehicles (§41-1a-201, “**Registration required**”), and support orders (§81-8-610, “**Registration for modification**”). Calling all of these `/operate/register` would erase legally decisive object and effect; distributing them among purpose roots would erase their shared form.

Conversely, a single purpose can require legally noninterchangeable forms: creating a trust under §75B-2-401, delegating parental powers under §75-5-103, petitioning for annulment under §81-4-303, and obtaining insurance coverage under §31A-22-655, “**Living organ donor coverage**.” Purpose does not determine validity requirements, authorized actor, third-party effect, revocability, forum, or remedy.

The normative identifier should therefore be built primarily from **institutional form + legal effect + object**, such as `file/financing-statement`, `revoke/power-of-attorney`, or `appoint/guardian`. Telos can then annotate why the actor did it: protect, finance, govern, comply, or care.

The strongest counterargument is that users search and automate by goals, not doctrinal categories; a purpose hierarchy can make heterogeneous legal mechanisms discoverable. That is persuasive for navigation and service design, but not for act identity. The answer is a purpose facet or mapping layer, not making purpose the canonical parent of the legal act.

# 6. Omissions and Duties

Based on heading classification, an estimated **26–34% of the sample is principally about mandatory or prohibited behavior**, compared with approximately **10–15% principally granting permissions or powers**. The remainder defines terms, creates institutions, prescribes procedures, or states consequences. These are **heading-based estimates from the sample**, not clause-level counts; the source does not provide operative-text modal frequencies.

Mandatory examples include §53E-6-701, “**Mandatory reporting of physical or sexual abuse of students**”; §41-1a-201, “**Registration required -- Penalty**”; and §19-5-107, “**Discharge of pollutants unlawful -- Discharge permit required**.” Prohibitions include §20A-1-606, “**Wagering on elections forbidden**,” and §13-32a-110.5, “**Transactions with certain individuals prohibited**.” Operative omissions include §41-12a-511, “**Failure to satisfy judgment**,” §76-18-221, “**Unlawful failure to report**,” and §20A-11-403, “**Failure to file -- Penalties**.”

A taxonomy whose primitives represent only permissions cannot express a large part of legal normativity. It needs either modalized act identifiers or a separate normative layer covering **may, must, must not, is exempt, is immune, is liable, and incurs consequence upon omission**. Omissions must be first-class, because “fail to file” and “refuse a chemical test” are legally individuated conduct, not merely absent positive interactions.

# 7. Transferability Warning

## Likely artifacts of Utah and U.S. state law

The particular institutions and instruments are not universally portable: D.B.A. registration (§42-2-201); UCC financing statements and perfection (§70A-9a-516; §70A-9a-314); state initiative and referendum petitions (§20A-7-503; §20A-7-614); county and special-district structures (§17B-1-103, “**Special district status and powers**”); state professional licensure; trust deeds; Utah tax funds; and state-specific municipal annexation (§10-2-812). The section numbering, public-record architecture, common-law trust vocabulary, elected local offices, and division between state and federal authority are likewise local.

Whether a civil-law jurisdiction or the EU has exact functional equivalents for each Utah form is **NOT IN SOURCE**. One should not translate `financing-statement`, `D.B.A.`, `trust-deed`, or `initiative-petition` as universal acts without jurisdictional mappings.

## Findings likely to survive jurisdictional translation

The higher-order distinctions are more durable:

- laws name definitions, institutions, duties, prohibitions, procedures, sanctions, and allocations—not only acts;
- legal acts differ by authorized actor, formality, assent, revocability, temporal effect, and third-party effect;
- unilateral declarations, filings, elections, revocations, and omissions can have legal effect;
- institutional form is distinct from purpose;
- legal normativity requires mandatory and prohibitory modalities;
- procedures commonly have initiation, notice, decision, review, and enforcement stages.

These conclusions are visible across unlike Utah domains—for example, entity filing (§16-1a-206), adjudicative procedure (§63G-4-205), property perfection (§70A-9a-302), licensing revocation (§31A-16-115), and mandatory reporting (§53E-6-701). Exact labels will change by jurisdiction; the need for an effect-based, role-sensitive, modal legal-act model will not.
